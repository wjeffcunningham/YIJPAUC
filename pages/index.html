WHY IJPAUC?

Suppose that Steven Spielberg's mid-career magnum opus, Jurassic Park (1993), reflects the filmmaker's own guilt over his involvement in the illegal hiring of two children killed on a "Steven Spielberg Production" in 1982. Even if true, why would this discovery matter, all these years later, in today's far more cynical times?

WJC August 30th, 2026

In my seminal media essay, IJPAUC? (2025), I argue that Jurassic Park (1993) is an unconscious confession. By that I mean that when Spielberg's emphasis on the pertinence of autobiography to his work is taken seriously, the evidence is overwhelming that the '90s blockbuster bears heavy imprints of the notorious 1982 Twilight Zone: The Movie helicopter accident that took the lives of two illegally-hired child "extras," Myca Le (7), and Renee Chen (6).

I am not the first to make the connection between Jurassic Park (1993) and the Twilight Zone accident; I am, however, the first to ask whether it constitutes a "confession." In other words, I consider the cultural status of Jurassic Park in light of the unanswered—and indeed unasked—question of whether the film's co-producer, Spielberg, in any way (perhaps indirectly) sanctioned the illegal hiring.

So far, the question of assigning responsibility for the tragedy is eclipsed by the screaming, bullying maniac who'd been calling the shots that night, director John Landis, already a major figure in American film history. Yet someone else, orders of magnitude more significant to the cinema arts, is also implicated; or, at least, has been shielded from the question by those who were.

This question is motivated by the fact that Spielberg's Amblin' Entertainment co-founder, Frank Marshall, is deeply implicated in the illegal hiring, and evaded two subpoenas (one international) for his testimony, while spending most of 1983-1987 abroad making Amblin' movies. As well, virtually all production materials went missing. At the time, Spielberg was known for his prolific "thumbnail sketches," and for being a backseat director (e.g., on Tobe Hooper's Poltergeist, 1982).

In the three books written about the case (Special Effects, 1987; Outrageous Conduct, 1987; Fly by Night, 2022), each by reporters who sat in on the trial, the question of Spielberg's involvement in the planning of the fatal sequence remains one of plausible and serious interest. In short, the lion's share of the blame for the tragedy fell upon co-producer/writer/director John Landis: he, along with two production subordinates, was charged with two counts of manslaughter, one for each child. But Landis et al maintained that Spielberg's camp—the more experienced group of filmmakers, who had been tasked by the studio with keeping an eye on Landis—was aware of the illegal hiring and had given it their blessing. On close analysis, the facts largely support the Landis group's claim. As for Amblin', they've let the films do the talking. Most recently, Frank Marshall produced Jurassic World: Rebirth (2025).

The purpose of this supplementary essay is to explain the significance, or upshot, of my conclusions as to Spielberg's involvement based on the available evidence. I'll begin by recapping the accident and the argument for Spielberg's involvement in its particulars, before turning to what I see as the moral and poetic fallout of that involvement, given Spielberg's titanic status as an American poet.

In the end, do our narratives carry any real moral currency? And if so, upon what is that currency based?

  1. The Twilight Zone Accident (July 23rd, 1982) (tap to expand)

    At 2:20am, the two children are carried across a river—Le under one arm, Chen under the other—as the segment's protagonist, played by 53-year-old veteran Hollywood actor Vic Morrow (also killed), tries to deliver them to the safety of the opposite shore. But the fourth charge fired blew through the roof of a fishing hut, damaging the tail rotor of the helicopter, causing it to spiral downwards onto the spot where Morrow had just moments earlier stumbled to his knees. Chen had for a few brief seconds slipped from his grasp; plunged face-first into the murky waters below.

    Thanks to the diligent work of singular investigators in the NTSB and the Los Angeles D.A.'s office, charges of Involuntary Manslaughter were brought against five members of the film production, headlined by John Landis—the movie's co-producer and writer/director of the segment involving the children.

    Landis, along with two subordinates (his Associate Producer and Production Manager), admitted to soliciting the children without permits (a misdemeanour with a maximum $5,500 fine), to allow the production to work the children at night, for the sake of artistic effect, i.e., realism, which was entirely consistent with how they'd gone about making The Blues Brothers (1980). They denied that they saw any special danger in this despite the practical effects and pyrotechnics called for by the shot, because safety would be in the hands of trained professionals; the state challenged this account.

    The charge of Involuntary Manslaughter is commonly misunderstood. The state accepts that the defendant(s) did not intend the accident or the deaths. Since every defendant would maintain that the accident, being accidental, was beyond their control, the state must prove that it would have been foreseeable to a reasonable person in their same situation. In any case, however, what is being faulted is not the deaths per se, but the defendant(s)' character, as reflected by the state the defendant(s) had allowed it to be in at the time of their callous actions pertaining to the accident.

    After a long and contentious string of legal proceedings including a full criminal trial (1986-1987), the five defendants were finally acquitted in 1987. A $200-million lawsuit filed by the families in the days after the accident was settled for approximately $2-million per family during closing arguments.

    What does any of this have to do with Spielberg, who was only 35 years old at the time, and preoccupied with the release and reception of E.T.: The Extra-Terrestrial (July 1982)?

  2. Evidence of Amblin's Involvement (June/July 1982) (tap to expand)

    Twilight Zone: The Movie (released in 1983) was an anthology film co-produced by Landis and Spielberg. It featured four different segments by four different directors, of which Landis's—an original, written by Landis, eventually titled simply 'Time Out'—was the first to go to production.

    Spielberg was among 14 defendants named in the civil case. The suit had been filed in the early days after the accident and cast a wide swath. Not only Spielberg—who directed the film's own second segment, "Kick the Can"—but Joe Dante and George Miller, who directed the remaining two segments, were also named, despite plainly having had nothing to do with the hiring or accident. In the wake of the accident, Spielberg distanced himself from Landis, and by most popular tellings acquitted himself admirably of whatever trivial guilt he had incurred by association with Landis.

    The question is: did Spielberg in any way know in advance that Landis intended to hire two children without permits in order to be able to film on location at night in proximity to industrial machine and pyrotechnics?

    And would this not explain Marshall's otherwise bewildering tolerance of Landis's flagrant employment of minors in proximity to obvious illegality and danger? And Spielberg's continued association with Marshall, not to mention two other Amblin' contractors also deeply implicated in the illegal hiring, who all received promotions in the years after the verdict (1987)?

    Landis and his two "office" co-defendants—who were on the hook for two counts of Involuntary Manslaughter for the hiring alone—maintained that Executive Producer Frank Marshall gave the explicit permission to pursue the illegal hiring.

    (Marshall, of course, being one third of Amblin' Entertainment, Inc., along with Spielberg, and Kathleen Kennedy.) The defendants said that Marshall said that Kennedy had called the labor board to see whether it would be possible to get waivers allowing the children to work at night legally.

    This was no small claim: it was a plausible, unchallenged, exonerating narrative (for these counts, for these defendants). These letters resulted in the DA issuing a second, international, subpoena for Marshall, to no avail. Landis would go on to testify (January 1987) that Marshall gave him explicit permission to pursue the illegal hiring.

    Years later, Marshall denied ever having anything of significance to say. D.A. Gilbert Garcetti, however, made clear that the state made "every effort possible to subpoena Frank Marshall but he was able to get out of our hands." Kennedy's never had reason to speak on the matter. Both Marshall and Kennedy were stopped in early 1987 following an Interpol order but by then their testimony was of no use.

    Of course, Landis and his co-defendants are biased witnesses. So what do the facts tell us?

    Marshall's signature was on the cheque used to hire the children for the first day of filming (with a dispensation of cash from the Beverly Hills Bank of Los Angeles, care of Landis's mother, Shirley Levine, a teller at the bank, whose initials appear on the back of the cheque). A production assistant told the police that "the day of the accident or the day before," Marshall's accountant, working out of Amblin's offices, handed her an envelope, which the PA later observed to be full of $100-bills. This would account for the second night's pay for an unexpected additional "roll-over" day, which the parents hadn't been informed about until around 3:00am that morning; Marshall was allegedly involved in this decision as well.

    Marshall was on-set the night before the accident, where close-ups were being taken of the children well into the early morning hours of the next day and was alleged to have been consulted about scheduling decisions at that time. He was on-set at 9:30pm the night of the accident, when the children were placed in close proximity to explosives, and other obvious safety hazards.

    At the time of the final shot (2:20am), Marshall was standing beside the Locations Manager (Richard Vane). Vane testified that "Marshall turned to [me] and said 'Those are dolls he's picking up, aren't they?'" Vane claimed that both he and Marshall were under the impression real children were not going to be used for the 2:20am "finale." But is this plausible? Vane testified that he had been participating in an on-set conspiracy to conceal the presence of the two children from a particular Fire Safety Officer known also to work also as a Children's Welfare Advocate.

    So if Marshall is seriously implicated in the illegal hiring, then to what extent if any does that imply Spielberg's awareness of what was happening?

    Spielberg's only ever given a few highly-curated public statements on the matter, including a short response to the NTSB: "In response to your request, I was never at the Indian Dunes location of Twilight Zone on the night of the accident or at any other time. I declare under penalty of perjury that the foregoing is true and correct, executed at Los Angeles, California, this first day of December, 1982" directed at an NTSB request for clarification after a truck driver gave sworn testimony that, on the night of the accident, Spielberg was on set and had asked him to procure him a car. (The driver later said he must have mistaken Marshall for Spielberg.)

    At the time, Spielberg had developed a reputation for being professionally overbearing. He was contemporaneously (June 1982) involved in a public relations battle over his supposed micro-management of Tobe Hooper's Poltergeist set (in 1981). (Rather like the way Lucas hovered over Spielberg's shoulder on Raiders; just making sure he got it right.) He'd just been fined by the DGA for a trailer which showed Spielberg's name three or four times the sizes of Hooper's, on the end credits card of a trailer that it's unlikely the studio would have released without Spielberg's approval.

    Spielberg is well-documented by the era's fan magazines and "The Making Of..." documentaries (ironically often produced by Frank Marshall) to have been a prolific storyboarder around this time; filling album after album with cartoon sketches, which would later be condensed by illustrators into production storyboards. However, neither the production's storyboards for the segment, nor almost any other production evidence was ever turned over to investigators. The morning after the accident, Marshall was documented by Landis's secretary as raiding their production offices for various documents and files.

    If Spielberg's group was giving feedback to Landis's group through the intermediary Warner Bros.' Executive production apparatus(Namely, Production Executive, Lucy Fisher, who was Warner Bros.' chief liaison with Amblin' at the time), in cognizance of the directors' personal relationships, and that feedback is reflected in either the (3rd) draft of Landis's script, in which the rescue segment is introduced, or the (4th) draft of Landis's script, which adds a few subtle notes, namely the addition of a simple new prop, which ended up being gripped by the little girl in the final shot: a "broken and naked Barbie Doll."

    If Landis's motives are known to have been creative, then why not Spielberg's?

    In 1985, during an interview for The Color Purple, CNN's Sandy Kenyon asked Spielberg if he had any comment on the upcoming trial. Spielberg waved his hands and asked that the cameras be turned off.

    Lest the very suggestion be disregarded, Amblin', at the time, had itself an alarming history of child endangerment, including incidents on Jaws (1975) and E.T. (1982), along with several examples of carelessness and brutality towards stuntpersons, 1975-1982.

  3. The Basis of Hope in Trust
    But they [the historian and the poet] differ in this: the one speaks of what has come to be while the other speaks of what... would come to be. Therefore poiēsis is more philosophic and of more stature than history. For poetry speaks rather of the general things while history speaks of the particular things.
    Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 9 — 1451a36–b11, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

    Today we often hear the word "narrative"—as in, "controlling the narrative"—the narrative being a kind of story.

    But what exactly is a "story"? Why do "narratives" hold such power for us? And what are the limits of this power?

    The dominant paradigm of storytelling in the West is Joseph Campbell's "The Hero's Journey." According to Campbell, the essential parameters of storytelling can be derived by way of cross-cultural analysis. In short, a hero-protagonist leaves their village, slays the dragon, and returns home changed and triumphant. It's by being formatted in this way that stories "work": by modelling for spectators the attitude needed to overcome their own personal dragons, whatever they may be. By Campbell's presentation, storytelling's underlying civic virtue is its pro-social orientation: it is fixedly constructive or optimistic.

    But for Aristotle, who in c. 335 BCE wrote his Poetics—the earliest available Western treatise on storytelling—Campbell's method does not adequately account for the effects of story in their sharpest expressions.

    Story then is the first principle and like the soul of tragedy.
    Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 6 — 1450a38–39, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

    For Aristotle, a story (μῦθος) is that which organizes the incidents of tragedy specifically—the dramatic form developed in ancient Athens, which peaked over a century before Aristotle started writing about it. Since the paradigmatic story only emerges as a late historical development, Campbell's strategy of establishing the form by way of cultural aggregate won't do. For Aristotle, any account of story has to convincingly explain how a tragedy makes a spectator burst into tears.

    Tragedy then gradually evolved as men developed each element that came to light, and after going through many changes, it stopped when it had found its own natural form.
    Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 4 — 1449a14–15, trans. Sachs Perseus ↗

    To say, however, that for Aristotle, the paradgimatic story is "tragic," is not to say that stories which end in suffering are preferred or primary. It's not that the best stories are tragic; it's that the tragic form best illustrates how stories work for human-beings. Arisotle shows great deference to comedy, which he argues "came to light side by side" with tragedy (1449a). Besides that, even within the form of tragedy, some tragedies have happy endings; Aristotle repeatedly sets Sophocles' Oedipus Tyrannus (429 BCE)—a tragedy in the typical sense—against Euripides' Iphigenia at Tauris (414 BCE), in which the protagonists recognize each other in time to avert the fatal mistake.

    All of this is to say that what is at stake (in Campbell vs Aristotle) is not a favoured valence or framing of events. Aristotle emphasizes that effective story depends on τὸ πεπλεγμένον [complexity], the successful execution of moments of "reversal" (περιπέτεια) and "recognition" (ἀναγνώρισις). These are the critical twists which suddenly put the spectator into a position of feeling empathy for those for whom this reaction would normally be blocked—such as for those who are culpable for the suffering they have wrought.

    The upshot of Aristotle's emphasis on dynamics over sequence is the ability to account for ethical dimensions of story entirely transparent to Campbell's analysis.

    Ironically, many Campbellians consider themselves as following in Aristotle's tradition. They overstate his principle that a story has a beginning, middle, and end.
    1. 3a. On Aristotle's Poetics & Rhetoric (tap to expand)(tap to collapse)

      Aristotle's claim hinges on what he calls the ἴδιον (defining quality) of tragedy: its capacity, "through pity and fear," to induce κάθαρσις (catharsis) of these same feelings. (What is meant precisely by "catharsis" in this context is a matter of controversy, to be discussed below.)

      Spectator reactions to tragedy were sharp and conspicuous. "Pity (ἔλεος) and fear (φόβος)" had already been theorized at the time to be germane to both tragedy and rhetoric.

      The problem becomes salient once one appreciates that pity and fear are almost opposites, and from many angles drive each other out. Aristotle defines the two terms in the Rhetoric. Fear concerns our anticipation of our own potential suffering. Pity concerns our anticipation of the potential suffering of others who are like us—as long as they don't deserve it. (Rhet 2.5; 2.7; Poetics 13.) It is very difficult to pity someone that you fear at the same time as you are fearing them. It's this intersubjective asymmetry—the lack of a "doesn't deserve it" clause on fear, in contrast to pity—which tragedy exploits.

      Although Aristotle establishes in Ch. 6, he only describes its mechanisms progressively, by way of argument, through the text.

      1. Ch. 9: Wonder (θαυμαστόν) and Beauty (καλόν) (tap to expand)(tap to collapse)
        Fearful and pitiable things come to be… especially… whenever on account of one another they come to be contrary to expectation. For if this is the case, it will be more wondrous (θαυμαστὸν) than if they come to be spontaneously or by chance, since even among chance things those seem most wondrous (θαυμασιώτατα) which appear to have come to be as if for a purpose. For example, the statue of Mitys at Argos killed the one who was the cause of the death of Mitys; it fell on him while he contemplated it. For it is likely that as things of this sort do not come to be at random, so stories of this sort are of necessity more beautiful (καλλίους).
        Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 9 — 1452a1–11, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

        In this passage, Aristotle provides what feels like a tragic plot in miniature. The point is to suggest how pity and fear might be produced together “whenever on account of one another they come to be contrary to expectation.”

        The strangeness of this construction warrants articulation. Pity caused by fear, and fear caused by pity. They (the pity-and-fear construct) should come to be, not randomly; but contrary to expectation.

        Not because it can't happen randomly, but because this uncanny simultaneity is more wondrous to the extent that it "appear[s] to have come to be as if for a purpose."

        What is the significance of Aristotle’s emphasis on apparent teleology? What or whose "purpose" does he mean?

      2. Ch. 11: Reversal (περιπέτεια), Recognition (ἀναγνώρισις), Suffering (πάθος) (tap to expand)(tap to collapse)

        Reversal and recognition, says Aristotle, besides the story (the poet's sequencing of events), "are the greatest things by which tragedy 'guides the soul' (ψυχαγωγεῖ) and specifically characterize a "complex" story against a "simple" one.

        Aristotle gives as an example of reversal from Sophocles' Oedipus:

        What has been spoken of as the change into the contrary of the things being done is reversal (περιπέτεια), and this too, just as we say, according to the likely or necessary. For example in the Oedipus, on his coming [the messenger from Corinth], in supposing that he was going to cheer Oedipus and release him from the fear regarding his mother, once he had made it clear who he was, he did the contrary.
        Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 11 — 1452a24-28, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

        And an explanation of recognition, also from the Oedipus:

        Recognition (ἀναγνώρισις), on the other hand, just as the name too signifies, is a change from ignorance to knowledge, whether toward friendship or enmity, of those whose relation to good or ill fortune has already been determined. A recognition (ἀναγνώρισις) is most beautiful when it comes to be at the same time as a reversal (περιπετείᾳ), for example as it is in the Oedipus.
        Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 11 — 1452a30-34, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

        Reversal is a change in the; e.g., to unveil the source of the plague (as in Oedipus). Until this point in the play, everything has been about discovering that source; now it's going in a whole other direction.

        The recognition is the epistemic adjustment to that state. The distinction might seem academic because in the case of Oedipus—but not necessarily all tragedies, as Aristotle points out—they occur at the same time.

        When knowledge of the reversal is the recognition, it means the action has been done.

        Aristotle also tags, as a third part of the story, the implied terminal point, the unhappy outcome of the change, the suffering.

        These, then, are two parts of the story, reversal (περιπέτεια) and recognition (ἀναγνώρισις), and a third is suffering (πάθος). Of these, reversal (περιπέτεια) and recognition (ἀναγνώρισις) have been spoken of, but suffering (πάθος) is a destructive or painful action such as killings in the open, and the infliction of excessive pains and wounds and everything of this sort.
        Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 11 — 1452b10-15, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

        This always looms in the background. When reversal and recognition come to be simultaneously; i.e., information delivered to the protagonist, which changes the scenario, when it's already too late to do anything about it but suffer.

        But knowledge of the reversal might happen with half of the recognition still pending, with a range of dramatic outcomes still viable, some in good fortune, some in bad.

        At this point, Aristotle raises a key counter-example to Sophocles' Oedipus: Euripides' Iphigenia at Tauris. Iphigenia at Tauris belonged to a class of escape-tragedies pioneered by Euripides, ending in good fortune. Aristotle conveniently provides .

        Ergo, vis-à-vis the analysis Aristotle is developing, whether the paradigmatic tragedy ends in good or bad fortune is an open question—not foreclosed for bad fortune as one might presume. Tragedies could certainly have happy endings and still be effective, as Aristotle will explain in Chapter 14 in an apparent reversal of his own.

        In the example: Iphigenia reads aloud a letter that reveals her identity to the two men standing in front of her. One of the men is her beleagured, long-lost brother, Orestes, who now recognizes her for who she is; kidnapped, and put into the role of priestess. But she has not yet recognized him; and he can't say it out loud, because now they're surrounded by hostile Taurians. This leaves open the possibility that she sacrifices him before she recognizes him for who he is. The fate of Orestes' friend, Pylades, also hangs in the balance.

        In Euripides' play, all three manage to get away safely.

        In these cases the "suffering" aspect may only play out within the protagonist's fears—reasonably inferred and anticipated by the spectator—whether or not they actually come to pass.

        Aristotle writes that "a recognition is most beautiful when it comes to be at the same time as a reversal, for example as it is in the Oedipus."

        But why? Why not as it is in the Iphigenia?

      3. Ch. 13: Mistake (ἁμαρτία), Decency (ἐπιείκεια), Loathsome (μιαρόν), Philanthropic Sentiment (φιλάνθρωπον) (tap to expand)(tap to collapse)

        Since recognition and reversal concern mistakes, and mistakes are made by people acting, the mistake that induces the moments of recognition and reversal depends on someone making it. But not just any arc will produce the tragic effect.

        Aristotle names a few that won't work. In considering the reasons why, as though generating elemental sparks by way of particle acceleration, we gain a new handful of terms applicable to the ongoing dileneation of the paradigmatic story with its dependence on the generation of "pity and fear" and catharsis thereby.

        1. The Downfall of the Decent Man (ἐπιεικής) (tap to expand)(tap to collapse)
          ...the ἐπιεικής ought not to be shown changing from good to bad fortune (for this is neither fearful nor pitiable but μιαρόν)...
          Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 13 — 1452b28, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

          The term ἐπιεικής is discussed the Nicomachean Ethics, Book V, in contrast to the law-abiding man per se:

          …while what is decent is just, it is not so according to the law, but is a setting straight of what is legally just.
          Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Ch. V.10 — 1137b26, trans. Sachs Perseus ↗

          Instead, Aristotle describes the arc in which the decent man comes to ruin would be experienced by spectators as μιαρόν (repulsive).

          Aren't terrible things happening to decent men the epitome of tragedy? No; Aristotle claims that such a plot wouldn't produce fear or pity, but μιαρόν: disgust.

          But why this limit, exactly? ἐπιεικής?

          Throughout each of Arisotle's excluded plots, a repeated problem is spectator identification.

        2. The Success of the Wicked Man (tap to expand)(tap to collapse)
          ...so the wicked ought not to be shown changing from misfortune to good fortune (for this is the least tragic of all, since it has nothing of what it ought to have as it is neither productive of philanthropic feelings (φιλάνθρωπον) nor pitiable nor fearful)...
          Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 13 — 1452b30, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

          An evil man coming out well could loosely be said to be "tragic," in the sense of being a bad outcome, but Aristotle points out that this would not trigger pity, since:

          …with respect to one who has ill fortune, the pity concerns his not deserving it, and the fear concerns his being similar to us, so that what occurs will be neither pitiable nor fearful.
          Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 13 — 1452b30, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

          No one identifies with a truly "bad guy" in the first place. But notably, even when the bad guy, say, gets away with it, we're not as disgusted in this same way as we would be to see the ἐπιεικής come to bad fortune by way of a reversal.

        3. The Downfall of the Wicked Man (tap to expand)(tap to collapse)

          Aristotle notes that in this case, φιλανθρωπία [love of humanity] would be produced but without pity or fear. In narrative like this, even when there's a "twist of fate," the gratification is different; what's generated for the spectator is more like a shared love for humankind than it is the tragic effect we have been locating.

          And friendship seems to be present by nature in a parent for a child and in a child for a parent, not only in human beings but also in birds and most animals, and for animals alike in kin toward one another, and especially among human beings, which is why we praise those who are friends of humanity (τοὺς φιλανθρώπους). And one might see among those who travel that every human being is akin and a friend to a human being.
          Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Ch. VIII.1 — 1155a16–22, trans. Sachs Perseus ↗

          This rules out justified comeuppance as the mechanism of the tragic effect.

          None of these three plots work: "decent" guy loses; "bad guy wins"; "bad guy loses".

          One key point, here, again, is the intersubjective gap between fear and pity fleshed out in the Rhetoric: our ability to fear for ourselves, despite dimensions of culpability which might preclude our being pitied by others. With almost optical precision, Aristotle is delimiting the psychic field at issue: actions for which we'd forgive ourselves but for which we'd hestitate to forgive others.

          In Ch. 13, in assessing a suitable tragic hero, Aristotle argues that a ["very wicked"--translate] man won't do, because "…with respect to one who has ill fortune, the pity concerns his not deserving it, and the fear concerns his being similar to us, so that what occurs will be neither pitiable nor fearful."

          Aristotle introduced the spectator as a term of concern to the analysis of tragedy at 1459a6-9. The spectator must "identify" somewhat with the tragic protagonist in order for the tragic effect to take place; as the protagonist completes their change of fortune, by way of this identification, somehow, the effect will take place.

          The spectator begins by regarding the protagonist with vague, generalized good-will, the same way one might spectate a favoured athlete. This vague good-will is enabled by the initial excluding conditions Aristotle advises.

          "The fear concerns his being similar to us:" how?

        He who remains:

        …is neither distinguished by virtue and justice nor changing to bad fortune on account of vice and wickedness, but one who changes on account of some mistake (ἁμαρτίαν τινά), and is one of those in great repute and of good fortune, such as Oedipus, Thyestes, and notable men of families of this sort.
        Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 13 — 1453a7, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

        It's only at this point that Aristotle concludes the change of fortune must come by way of a ἁμαρτία (mistake) and not wickedness.

        Hamartia (Ancient Greek: ἁμαρτία) originally means something like “missing the mark." With Christianity, it acquires the moral and theological charge of “sin.” But here, to be clear, being off the mark can in some cases very much reflect a genuine fault or defect of character.

        The point is that audiences are moved not by the good or bad guy winning or losing per se but by the rush of adrenaline all of us get when we think we may have just messed everything up.

        It is necessary, then, for the beautiful story to be simple rather than, as some say, double, and to change not to good fortune from bad fortune but the opposite, from good fortune to bad fortune, not because of wickedness but because of a [mistake] (ἁμαρτίαν μεγάλην)…
        Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 13 — 1453a8, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

        This confirms Sophocles' Oedipus as a paradigmatic tragedy (example of the "most beautiful"), and Oedipus a paradigmatic tragic hero. Aristotle also mentions Thyestes; Orestes' grandfather, who was made to eat his own children in a soup.

      4. Ch. 14: Recognition of What Could Have Been (tap to expand)(tap to collapse)

        In Chapter 14, Aristotle concerns himself with the most beautiful kind of recognition.

        What more, however, is there to say?

        In Chapter 11, Aristotle wrote that “A recognition is most beautiful when it comes to be at the same time as a reversal, for example as it is in the Oedipus.”

        In Chapter 13—just previously—Aristotle reasoned that the tragic protagonist is “one who changes on account of some mistake and is one of those in great repute and of good fortune such as Oedipus, Thyestes, and notable men of families of this sort. … It is necessary, then, for the beautiful story… to change… from good fortune to bad fortune, not because of wickedness but because of a great mistake…”

        He goes as far as to diagnose the cause of poets’ tendency toward happy endings—pandering.

        For the poets follow the spectators, making their poetry as they [the spectators] would pray for things to be. But this is not a pleasure of tragedy but is rather proper to comedy, where those who are the greatest of enemies in the story, for example, Orestes and Aegisthus, having become friends, go off at the end, and no one is killed by anyone.
        Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 13 — 1453a35–40, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

        The dimension of analysis introduced in this chapter stems from Aristotle’s premise in Ch. 11 that “recognition is recognition of some [persons]”—that is, recognition in the strong sense required by the tragic effect is not merely of something, but of someone.

        In Ch. 14, Aristotle divides this distinction further, splitting the object of recognition from “some persons,” into friends or enemies:

        It is surely necessary that actions of this sort be in the relation of friends toward one another, or of enemies, or of neither. Should it be enemy toward enemy, he is neither doing nor intending anything piteous except by virtue of the suffering itself, any more than if they are neither friends nor enemies. But the sufferings to be sought are these: whether they come to be in friendships, such as if brother should either kill or intend to kill or do something else of this sort to brother, or son to father, or mother to son, or son to mother.
        Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 14 — 1453b15–22, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

        This distinction appears to reveal a dramatic possibility hitherto overlooked, vindicating the happy outcome.

        This is because Aristotle observes that the act does not need to occur for its resonances to be felt by the audience.

        The tragic action can be done knowingly (like in Medea, when she plots to, and does, kill her children) or unknowingly (like in Oedipus).

        Of the two, Aristotle favours the latter; which is a complex plot, allowing for an “astounding” recognition.

        But he also considers a third possibility: “to intend to do something beyond cure because of ignorance but to recognize before doing it”—like in the Iphigenia.

        Even worse than the Medea structure, Aristotle says, would be a variant in which the murderous action is intended but never happens. This would be μιαρόν (disgusting) to see—presumably because the ill-will is never surfaced, and there’s no resolution.

        To intend while cognizant and not to act is worst, for it is loathsome and not tragic, since it is without suffering.
        Aristotle, Poetics, Ch. 14 — 1453b35–36, trans. Benardete/Davis Perseus ↗

        By the same token, however, a variant of the Oedipus, in which the unintended action never comes to pass prior to the recognition, would be stronger than the Oedipus.

        While the Medea-variant suffers from the audience’s imagining of the deed; the Oedipus-variant benefits from it, anticipating the potential suffering enough for the tragic effect to work, but with the story still ending “as they would pray for things to be.”

        The uncanny upshot of this conclusion is that apparently the paradigmatic story isn’t Sophocles’ Oedipus, but Euripides’ Iphigenia, as a “positive” variant of the former, using its same engine.

      5. The Tragic Effect—Catharsis (κάθαρσις) (tap to expand)(tap to collapse)

        By this point the tragic mechanism can be inferred.

        [[And friendly affection goes together with the habit of living together, whereas goodwill arises suddenly—for example, it even arises for competitors, since people come to have goodwill for competitors and share their intent, though they would do nothing to assist them. For, just as we said, people feel goodwill suddenly and so feel only superficial affection. (EN 1166b34-1167a6ff.)]]

        Recall the Mitys outline: the one who was the cause of the death of Mitys changes from good to bad fortune (death) by way of a mistake.

        By the time the reversal happens, it's too late, and the suffering (being crushed) is imminent.

        But which mistake? Thinking in front of the wrong statue? No; it must be earlier. Which mistake belong to Mitys's killer? The killing of Mitys? Had he killed Mitys intentionally, or accidentally?

        Whatever the mistake, the hero must be at least partially culpable for it, for the tragic engine to produce sufficient torque. This is to say: given the power of the tragic effect, it can only be explained by a shift in the preceived culpability of the protagonist for their fate (cf. Sachs). This would explain how fear and pity could be held in parity.

        This matches the hamartia of Poetics 13 with the hamartema of Nicomachean Ethics V.8, which distinguishes between mistakes in general, and culpable mistakes (e.g., acts of negligence, like throwing a spear carelessly and hitting someone).

        The spectator begins by identifying with a hero, in a similar way to how they'd identify with an athlete at a sporting event. (EN 1166b34–1167a6ff.; 1170b6–15. Friendship (φιλία [friendship]) models the self's relation to others: "all the things that have to do with friendship arise out of oneself and extend to others" (1168b3–7). To judge that someone is a φίλος [friend] is to be aware that their being is similar to one's own (1170b7–13). A philos, then, provides a second self—but one whose ways of being-at-work can be observed from a detached perspective not easily attained with regard to one's own being (1169b34–36).)

        And yet, we pity them. Why? Because we already identified with them, so that we could pity them, so that we could feel their fear, from a safe distance. After the reversal.

        Only, that is, if the sudden adjustment from non-culpable to culpable obtains in this specific sense.

        Normally, Aristotle tells us, pity is limited by the sufferer's not deserving their suffering (Rhet 2.8). But something in tragedy causes us to reckon that perspective against our own identification with the protagonist—would that our circumstances were the same.

        For the protagonist to trigger the tragic effect, they would have to be in some way culpable: much like the defendant in a case of involuntary manslaughter, who never meant to do what he did, but it happened anyway, because he was in some way negligent relative to an expected standard of conduct. This is a mistake in the specific sense defined by Aristotle at Nicomachean Ethics V.8.

        And this realization is the occasion for the cathartic expression we associate with tragedy. Couldn't the same happen to me, if I were justly punished for my worst lapses—had the worst possible or probable consequence of those lapses come to pass? Might there be outcomes still? Would I then be worthy of any pity at all? In the moment of reversal/recognition, our daily, oscillating tendencies of blame and forgiveness are momentarily suspended, and we see things, for a moment, in a personally significant reflection upon the whole.

        "At what point could I be forgiven? At what point is it just a matter of time?"

        "Was I too loose? Was I too tight? What could have been, what if not for… What might be yet to come, unless I'm diligent and prepared…"

        This node of human anxieties—those of the ἀκρατής (unrestrained) person, more or less—is what gets projected onto stories and salved by tragedy, in due proportion to the matter concerned, ranging from day-to-day worries to full-blown mania.

        For the political extension of this question, see Aristotle’s Politics.

      To what extent is narrative optimism a narcotic? Given that Hollywood film typically has obvious political significances and relevancies, fathoming the relevance of this question requires the rhetorical lens.

      In On Rhetoric, Aristotle argues that those who write about the science of persuasion neglect the role of the speaker's perceived character in its function.

      …for it is not in the way some of the speech-art makers assume in their arts, that the decency of the speaker contributes nothing to his persuasiveness, but character, one might say, has in it just about the most decisive means of persuasion.
      Aristotle, On Rhetoric, Ch. I.2 — 1356a1–13, trans. Sachs Perseus ↗

      This is relevant to our question insofar as Hollywood movies are not only a form of storytelling, but also a kind of "display speech" (ἐπίδειξις)—the branch of rhetoric concerned not with what's advantageous (the future, proper to political deliberation) or what's just (the past, proper to the courtroom), but with what's beautiful or shameful, judged in the present.

      Both storytelling and display speech, says Aristotle, traffic in representing the beautiful, even where their material is drawn from anxieties about the past and future.

      Aristotle counts among the means of persuasion not only the argument itself, and the state of mind the listeners are put into, but also the character of the speaker. Considering the character of the speaker, Aristotle says three things matter:

      These are judgement, virtue, and goodwill. For people go wrong in the things they speak of or give advice about by reason of all these, or of any one of them, since they either have incorrect opinions on account of a lack of judgement, or, while having correct opinions, do not say what seems true to them on account of vice, or, if they are of good judgment and decent but not good-willed, it is possible for that very reason for them to fail to give the best advice they know how to give; besides these there are none. It is a necessity, therefore, that anyone who seems to have all these attributes will be trusted by his hearers.
      Aristotle, On Rhetoric, Ch. II.1 — TK, trans. Sachs Perseus ↗

      Given that popular entertainment falls under the category of ἐπίδειξις—performance speech concerned with praising or blaming with regard to "what is beautiful or shameful," bringing other things back to refer to these—its presently-focused social function is telling people what to think of the future, or of the past.

      The obvious problem is that when a speaker's lived behaviour contradicts the ethical implications of his speech (should they exist), the former is at least a factor in the spectatorship of the work.

      The extreme version of this is the appeal made to a jury to impeach a witness's account:

      But if there is a contradictory oath sworn to by one's opponent, one may argue that someone who does not stand by things he has sworn to makes everything null and void, and it is for this reason that those who apply the laws have taken oaths: They think it right for all of you to stand by the oaths you've sworn when you judge them, when they themselves do not stand by theirs.
      Aristotle, On Rhetoric, Ch. I.15 — 1377b9–12, trans. Sachs Perseus ↗

      If a speaker's credibility is bankrupted, then, at least, that aspect or meta-aspect of their poem changes in light of that fact. For Aristotle, the poetic and the rhetorical are dual objects; one reflects the other in all but the purest viewing contexts — but particularly in respect of the moral dimensions of the object; this is the axis of dissonance. But it emanates to the rest in the form of a dilapidation (referred to as a sort of μίαρον in Sophocles' Oedipus Rex).
