WHY IJPAUC?

Suppose that Steven Spielberg's mid-career magnum opus, Jurassic Park (1993), reflects the filmmaker's own guilt over his involvement in the illegal hiring of two children killed on a "Steven Spielberg Production" in 1982. Even if true, why would this discovery matter, all these years later, in today's far more cynical times?

WJC May 1st, 2026 VVIP26

In my seminal media essay, IJPAUC? (2025), I argue that Jurassic Park (1993) is an unconscious confession. By that I mean that: when Spielberg's emphasis on the pertinence of autobiography and psychoanalyis to his work is taken seriously, the evidence is overwhelming that the franchise-founding '90s blockbuster bears heavy imprints of the infamous 1982 Warner Bros' Twilight Zone: The Movie helicopter accident that took the lives of two immigrant children knowingly hired by the production as unpermitted "extras," Myca Le (7), and Renee Chen (6).

I am not the first to make the connection between Jurassic Park (1993) and the Twilight Zone accident; I am, however, the first to ask whether it constitutes a "confession." In other words, I consider the cultural status of Jurassic Park in light of the unanswered—and indeed, unasked—question of whether Spielberg in any way (tacitly or explicitly) sanctioned the illegal hiring. This question is motivated by the facts that Spielberg's Amblin Entertainment Co-Founder, Frank Marshall, is deeply implicated in the illegal hiring, and avoided two subpoenas (one international) for his testimony in 5 years abroad. As well, virtually all of the segment's production materials went missing. At the time, Spielberg was notorious for his prolific "thumbnail sketches," and for being a back-seat director (e.g. on Tobe Hooper's Poltergeist, 1982).

In the three books written about the case (Special Effects, 1987; Outrageous Conduct, 1987; Fly by Night, 2022), each by reporters who sat in on the trial, the question of Spielberg's involvement in the planning of the fatal sequence remains one of plausible and serious interest. In short, the lion's share of the blame for the tragedy fell upon co-producer/writer/director John Landis: he along with two production subordinates were charged with two counts of manslaughter, one for each child. But they maintained that co-producer Spielberg's camp—the more experienced group of filmmakers, who had been tasked by the studio with keeping an eye on Landis)—were well-aware of the illegal hiring and had given it their blessing. On close analysis, the facts of the matter largely support the Landis group's claim. As for Amblin, they've let the films do the talking. Most recently, Frank Marshall produced Jurassic World: Rebirth (2025).

The purpose of this supplementary essay is to explain the significance, or upshot, of my conclusions as to Spielberg's involvement based on the available evidence. I'll begin by recapping the accident and the argument for Spielberg's involvement in its particulars, before turning to what I see as the moral and poetic fallout of that involvement, given Spielberg's titanic status as an American poet.

In the end, do our narratives carry any real moral currency? And if so, upon what is that currency based?

  1. The Twilight Zone Accident (July 23rd, 1982) (tap to expand)

    At 2:20am, the two children are carried across a river—Le under one arm, Chen under the other—as the anti-hero, played by 53-year-old veteran Hollywood actor Vic Morrow (also killed), tries to deliver them to the safety of the opposite shore. But the fourth and fifth explosions fired blow through the roof of a fishing hut and destroy the tail rotor of the helicopter, causing it to spiral downwards onto the spot where Morrow had just moments earlier stumbled to his knees. Renee had for a few brief seconds slipped from his grasp; plunged face-first into the murky waters below.

    Thanks to the diligent work of singular investigators in the NTSB and the Los Angeles D.A.'s office, charges of Involuntary Manslaughter were brought against five members of the film production, headlined by John Landis, the movie's co-producer, and the writer/director of the segment involving the children.

    Landis, along with two subordinates (his Associate Producer and Production Manager), admitted to soliciting the children without permits (a misdemeanour with a maximum $5,500 fine) so as to allow the production to work the children at night for the sake of artistic effect, i.e., for the sake of realism, which was entirely consistent with how they'd gone about making The Blues Brothers (1980). They denied that they saw any special danger in this despite the practical effects and pyrotechnics called for by the shot, because safety would be in the hands of trained professionals; the state challenged this assertion.

    The charge of Involuntary Manslaughter is commonly misunderstood. The state accepts that the defendant(s) did not intend the accident or the deaths. Since every defendant would maintain that the accident, being accidental, was beyond their control, the state must prove that it would have been foreseeable to a reasonable person in their same situation. In any case, however, what is being faulted is not the deaths per se, but the defendant's character, as reflected by the state they had allowed themselves to be in at the time of their reckless actions pertaining to the accident.

    After a long and contentious string of legal proceedings including a full criminal trial (1986-1987), the five defendants were finally acquitted in 1987. A $200-million dollar lawsuit filed by the families in the days after the accident was settled for approx. $2-million dollars per family during closing arguments.

    What does any of this have to do with Spielberg, who was only 36-years-old at the time, and preoccupied with the release and reception of E.T.: The Extra-Terrestrial (July 1982)?

  2. Evidence of Amblin's Involvement (June/July 1982) (tap to expand)

    Twilight Zone: The Movie was an anthology film co-produced by Landis and Spielberg. It featured four different segments by four different directors, of which Landis's—an original, written by Landis, eventually titled simply 'Time Out'—was the first to go to production.

    Spielberg was among 14 defendants named in the civil case. But the suit had been filed in the early days after the accident and cast a wide swath. Not only Spielberg, but Joe Dante and George Miller—the directors of the other two segments—were named, who plainly had nothing to do with the hiring or accident. In the wake of the accident, Spielberg distanced himself from Landis who was previously a friend or friendly rival, and by popular telling acquitted himself admirably of whatever trivial association existed between himself and Landis.

    The question is: did Spielberg in any way know in advance that Landis intended to hire two children without permits in order to be able to film on location at night in proximity to industrial machine and pyrotechnics?

    And would this not explain Marshall's otherwise bewildering tolerance of Landis's flagrant employment of minors in proximity to obvious illegality and danger? And Spielberg's continued association with Marshall, not to mention two other Amblin' contractors also deeply implicated in the illegal hiring, who all received promotions in the years after the verdict (1987).

    Landis and his two "office" co-defendants—who were on the hook for two counts of Involuntary Manslaughter for the hiring alone—maintained that Executive Producer Frank Marshall gave the explicit permission to pursue the illegal hiring.

    Marshall of course being one third of Amblin, Entertainment, Inc., along with Spielberg, and Kathleen Kennedy. The defendants said that Marshall said that Kennedy had called the labor board to see whether it would be possible to get waivers allowing the children to work at night legally.

    Marshall denied having anything of significance to say. D.A. Gilbert Garcetti, however, made clear that the state made: "every effort possible to subpoena Frank Marshall but he was able to get out of our hands." Kennedy's never had reason to speak on the matter. Both Marshall and Kennedy were stopped on their way into Spain in early 1987, following an order by Interpol; but by that time Marshall's testimony was no longer of any use.

    Spielberg's given a few highly-curated public-facing statements, but nothing specific other than: "In response to your request, I was never at the Indian Dunes location of Twilight Zone on the night of the accident or at any other time. I declare under penalty of perjury that the foregoing is true and correct, executed at Los Angeles, California, this first day of December, 1982" directed at an NTSB request for clarification after a truck driver told them that, on the night of the accident, Spielberg was on set and had asked him to procure him a car. (The driver later said he must have mistaken Marshall for Spielberg.)

    Of course, Landis et al are as biased witnesses as we can imagine in this situation, hypothetically. So what do the facts of the matter tell us?

    Marshall's signature was on the cheque used to hire the children for the first day of filming (with a dispensation of cash from the Beverly Hills Bank of Los Angeles, care of Landis's mother, Shirley Levine, a teller at the bank, whose initials appear on the back of the cheque). A production assistant told the police that "the day of the accident or the day before," Marshall's accountant, working out of Amblin's offices, handed her an envelope, which the PA later observed to be full of $100-dollar bills. This would account for the second night's pay for an unexpected additional "roll-over" day, which the parents hadn't been informed about until around 3:00am that morning; Marshall was allegedly involved in this decision as well.

    Marshall was on-set the night before the accident, where close-ups were being taken of the children well into the early morning hours of the next day and was alleged to have been consulted about scheduling decisions at that time. He was on-set at 9:30pm the night of the accident, when the children were placed in close proximity to explosives, and other obvious safety hazards.

    At the time of the final shot (2:20am), Marshall was standing beside the Locations Manager, who testified at a grand jury in 1983 that he had been participating in an on-set conspiracy to conceal the presence of the two children from a particular Fire Safety Officer known also to work as an On-Set Teacher and Childrens' Welfare Advocate.   3. The Basis of Hope in Trust

In Aristotle's Rhetoric, he argues that those who write about the science of persuasion neglect the role of the speaker's perceived personal character in its function.

...for it is not in the way some of the speech-art makers assume in their "arts," that the decency of the speaker contributes nothing to his persuasiveness, but character, one might say, has in it just about the most decisive means of persuasion. (1356a, trans. Sachs).

The "problem" of this project can finally be described in its full dimensions:

The question of assigning responsibility for the Twilight Zone tragedy is eclipsed by the screaming, bullying, maniac who'd been calling the shots that night: director John Landis, already a major figure in American film history.

Yet someone else, orders of magnitude more significant to the cinema arts is also implicated; or, at least, has been shielded from the question by those who were.

Why have we so far been unable to ask or even "see" this question? And even if true, what does it mean or matter?

i. Aristotle On Poetics (tap to expand)
Tragedy then gradually evolved as men developed each element that came to light and after going through many changes, it stopped when it had found its own natural form. (1449a, trans. Sachs).

We can bring out the problem of narrative and morality by comparing Aristotle's Poetics and Rhetoric; one of which deals with poetic objects, like stories—the construction of which doesn't seem to depend on the poet's integrity, per se—and the other deals with rhetorical objects, i.e., politics-facing, where the speaker's integrity does matter. To what extent is a story also an act of political speech, and vice versa? In practice, how do the two forms intersect? To what extent can they be divorced?

On Poetics (350 BC) is Aristotle's book on storytelling. Much recommended by Aaron Sorkin.

What Aristotle means by story (μῦθος) is not the same as what it means to us today, acquainted as we are with the Hollywood format.

Aristotle's theory of storytelling differs from that of Joseph Campbell ("The Hero's Journey") insofar as Aristotle argues that the paradigmatic story only emerges as a later historical development, after much refinement; ergo, Campbell's strategy of reverse-engineering the archetypical template of story by general aggregate won't do; at least for an adequate explanation.

Arisotle's point hinges on what he calls the ἴδιον (or special "quality") of tragedy: its capacity, by way of fear and pity (he says), to induce relief or κάθαρσις of these emotions, along with feelings of θαυμάζειν (wonder or awe), and φιλανθρωπία (friendship in common with

all

humankind) (barbarous as they may be). (See, for example, Nicomachean Ethics 1161b5-7: "there seems to be something just for every human being toward all those who are capable of sharing in law and contractual agreement, and so there is friendship too, to the extent that he is a human being.")

In other words, a poetry with no shortage of civic benefits and which hardly depended on an ideal state. In this respect, Aristotle is what I would call a measured optimist.

Aristotle's optimism stands in contrast, not to Campbell's theory of storytelling per se, but to Nietzsche's, as detailed in The Birth of Tragedy Out of the Spirit of Song (1872). The delta here is not in Nietzsche's foregrounding of the tragic experience as the phenomenon worthy of analysis (in which the Dionysian cosmos is allowed a fleeting perspective of seeing itself seeing itself), but in his position (or assumption) that tragedy had ultimate priority among the various forms of storytelling for the Greeks.

But Aristotle never says this. While it's true that in Chapter 13 of the Poetics, Aristotle seems to say that the best tragic form is represented by tragedies that end as we expect tragedies to end.

...today the best tragedies are written about a few families—Alcmaeon for instance and Oedipus and Orestes and Meleager and Thyestes and Telephus and all the others whom it befell to suffer or inflict terrible disasters. Now, the most beautiful tragedy according to art is from this sort of putting-together. (1453a, trans. Sachs).

Aristotle then reverses himself in Chapter 14, where he offers a different ranking of the most beautiful tragic structure, favouring a new variant of tragedy pioneered by Euripides, in which the stories (like Iphigenia at Taurus) sometimes had happy or bittersweet endings. In this later ranking, Oedipus (and tragedies like it, where the protagonist only recognizes what they'd done after it's too late to save it) only ranks second place:

[...] But the strongest is the last; I mean for example, in the Cresphontes, Merope intends to kill her son and does not kill him but recognizes him, and in the Iphigenia, the sister goes through this with her brother, and in the Helle, the son, intending to betray his mother, recognizes her. (1454a, trans. Sachs).

This is all to say that, in terms of critical merit, Aristotle had no problem with a happy ending. It's just that the tragic form of storytelling especially displayed what makes all stories quite so powerful for human beings.

Aristotle's geneology of tragedy has it as a compressed form of epic; it achieves this compression by way of making the mythos "complex" rather than "simple." To do this it employed what Aristotle called the moments of reversal and recognition. All of this has some immediate, geometrical, implications, as it were; at least as Aristotle frames it. Since there are epic comedies, comedy (as such) appears as the compressed opposite to tragedy. And so, there would hardly be need to discuss anything but tragedy to understand the engine which in effect powered them all.

Fear was a basic emotion. As basic as desire. Pity was just fear on behalf of another, who did not deserve what was upon them. Even the best men, the decent and sensible men, knew fear, because of always having to stay vigilant for what could happen, if they weren't paying attention.

Story, then, is the first principle and like the soul of tragedy, and characters the second.

So all of this is about how best to construct stories for artistic effect. Should they have happy endings or sad? On this point Aristotle is agnostic. That is to say: the best narratives aren't always tragic. A happy ending isn't necessarily false. That's important to establish upfront, given some popular, some would say, intellectual, misconceptions. We shouldn't be adjusting for a bleak "ugly truth" made beautiful only in fleeting moments of delusion.

However, Aristotle cautioned against pandering; for that would turn every story into a comedy. In general, audiences preferred for stories to end as they would according to wish or prayer.

With that out of the way: what happens to "story" (mythos) when its speaker ceases to inspire; to be morally credible?
