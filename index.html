WHY IJPAUC?

Suppose that Steven Spielberg's mid-career magnum opus, Jurassic Park (1993), reflects the filmmaker's own guilt over his involvement in the illegal hiring of two children killed on a "Steven Spielberg Production" in 1982. Even if true, why would this discovery matter, all these years later, in today's far more cynical times?

WJC May 13th, 2026 VVIP26

In my seminal media essay, IJPAUC? (2025), I argue that Jurassic Park (1993) is an unconscious confession. By that I mean that when Steven Spielberg's emphasis on the pertinence of autobiography and psychoanalyis to his work is taken seriously, the evidence is overwhelming that the franchise-founding '90s blockbuster bears heavy imprints of the infamous 1982 Warner Bros' Twilight Zone: The Movie helicopter accident that took the lives of two illegally-hired "extras," Myca Le (7), and Renee Chen (6).

I am not the first to make the connection between Jurassic Park (1993) and the Twilight Zone accident; I am, however, the first to ask whether it constitutes a "confession." In other words, I consider the cultural status of Jurassic Park in light of the unanswered—and indeed, unasked—question of whether the film's co-producer, Spielberg, in any way (tacitly or explicitly) sanctioned the illegal hiring.

The question of assigning responsibility for the accident is eclipsed by the screaming, bullying, maniac who'd been calling the shots that night: director John Landis, already a major figure in American film history. Yet someone else, orders of magnitude more significant to the cinema arts is also implicated; or, at least, has been shielded from the question by those who were.

This question is motivated by the facts that Spielberg's Amblin Entertainment co-founder, Frank Marshall, is deeply implicated in the illegal hiring, and evaded two subpoenas (one international) for his testimony while spending most of 1983-1987 abroad making Amblin movies. As well, virtually all of the segment's production materials went missing. At the time, Spielberg was notorious for his prolific "thumbnail sketches," and for being a back-seat director (e.g. on Tobe Hooper's Poltergeist, 1982).

In the three books written about the case (Special Effects, 1987; Outrageous Conduct, 1987; Fly by Night, 2022), each by reporters who sat in on the trial, the question of Spielberg's involvement in the planning of the fatal sequence remains one of plausible and serious interest. In short, the lion's share of the blame for the tragedy fell upon co-producer/writer/director John Landis: he along with two production subordinates were charged with two counts of manslaughter, one for each child. But they maintained that Spielberg's camp—the more experienced group of filmmakers, who had been tasked by the studio with keeping an eye on Landis—were aware of the illegal hiring and had given it their blessing. On close analysis, the facts of the matter largely support the Landis group's claim. As for Amblin, they've let the films do the talking. Most recently, Frank Marshall produced Jurassic World: Rebirth (2025).

The purpose of this supplementary essay is to explain the significance, or upshot, of my conclusions as to Spielberg's involvement based on the available evidence. I'll begin by recapping the accident and the argument for Spielberg's involvement in its particulars, before turning to what I see as the moral and poetic fallout of that involvement, given Spielberg's titanic status as an American poet.

In the end, do our narratives carry any real moral currency? And if so, upon what is that currency based?

  1. The Twilight Zone Accident (July 23rd, 1982) (tap to expand)

    At 2:20am, the two children are carried across a river—Le under one arm, Chen under the other—as the segment's protagonist, played by 53-year-old veteran Hollywood actor Vic Morrow (also killed), tries to deliver them to the safety of the opposite shore. But the fourth and fifth explosions fired blow through the roof of a fishing hut and destroy the tail rotor of the helicopter, causing it to spiral downwards onto the spot where Morrow had just moments earlier stumbled to his knees. Chen had for a few brief seconds slipped from his grasp; plunged face-first into the murky waters below.

    Thanks to the diligent work of singular investigators in the NTSB and the Los Angeles D.A.'s office, charges of Involuntary Manslaughter were brought against five members of the film production, headlined by John Landis, the movie's co-producer, and the writer/director of the segment involving the children.

    Landis, along with two subordinates (his Associate Producer and Production Manager), admitted to soliciting the children without permits (a misdemeanour with a maximum $5,500 fine) so as to allow the production to work the children at night for the sake of artistic effect, i.e., for the sake of realism, which was entirely consistent with how they'd gone about making The Blues Brothers (1980). They denied that they saw any special danger in this despite the practical effects and pyrotechnics called for by the shot, because safety would be in the hands of trained professionals; the state challenged this account.

    The charge of Involuntary Manslaughter is commonly misunderstood. The state accepts that the defendant(s) did not intend the accident or the deaths. Since every defendant would maintain that the accident, being accidental, was beyond their control, the state must prove that it would have been foreseeable to a reasonable person in their same situation. In any case, however, what is being faulted is not the deaths per se, but the defendant's character, as reflected by the state they had allowed themselves to be in at the time of their reckless actions pertaining to the accident.

    After a long and contentious string of legal proceedings including a full criminal trial (1986-1987), the five defendants were finally acquitted in 1987. A $200-million dollar lawsuit filed by the families in the days after the accident was settled for approx. $2-million dollars per family during closing arguments.

    What does any of this have to do with Spielberg, who was only 36-years-old at the time, and preoccupied with the release and reception of E.T.: The Extra-Terrestrial (July 1982)?

  2. Evidence of Amblin's Involvement (June/July 1982) (tap to expand)

    Twilight Zone: The Movie (released in 1983) was an anthology film co-produced by Landis and Spielberg. It featured four different segments by four different directors, of which Landis's—an original, written by Landis, eventually titled simply 'Time Out'—was the first to go to production.

    Spielberg was among 14 defendants named in the civil case. But the suit had been filed in the early days after the accident and cast a wide swath. Not only Spielberg, but Joe Dante and George Miller—the directors of the other two segments—were named, who plainly had nothing to do with the hiring or accident. In the wake of the accident, Spielberg distanced himself from Landis who was previously a friend or friendly rival, and by most popular tellings acquitted himself admirably of whatever trivial association existed between himself and Landis.

    3. ii. Thucydides; The Melian Dialogue (tap to expand)
    The Basis of Hope in Trust In Aristotle's Rhetoric, he argues that those who write about the science of persuasion neglect the role of the speaker's perceived character in its function. ...for it is not in the way some of the speech-art makers assume in their "arts," that the decency of the speaker contributes nothing to his persuasiveness, but character, one might say, has in it just about the most decisive means of persuasion. (Rhet 1.2) This is relevant to our question insofar as Hollywood movies are not only a form of storytelling, but also a form of politically-oriented speech. Aristotle discusses storytelling in his Poetics (350 BCE)—the earliest known screenwriter’s manual—and speechmaking in his handbook on political speech, On Rhetoric, where he advances the thesis stated above. In short, when Aristotle’s theory of narrative is compared against his theory of persuasive speaking—we get a clear picture of the consequences of the collapse in moral credibility of a major imaginary object trafficking in any significant currency as a source of moral or aesthetic edification. [3a; On Aristotle on Poetics & Rhetoric] "Tragedy then gradually evolved as men developed each element that came to light and after going through many changes, it stopped when it had found its own natural form." (Poetics 1449a) Today we often hear the word “narrative”—as in, “controlling the narrative”—the narrative being a kind of story. But what exactly is a “story”? Why do “narratives” hold such power for us? And what are the limits of this power? What Aristotle means by story (μῦθος) is not the same as what we mean by it today, accustomed as we are to the Hollywood format, which has largely been templated according to the theories of Joseph Campbell and "The Hero's Journey.” According to Campbell, the essential parameters of storytelling can be derived by way of cross-cultural analysis. The common denominator is expressed as: A protagonist-figure leaves his village, slays the dragon, and returns home—a separation, initiation, return structure—with each moment and sub-moment of this journey available for amplification or thematization. Its bias is optimistic, insofar as it serves a dual cultural function: of modelling for its citizens the attitude needed to overcome their own personal dragons, whatever they may be. “Story then is the first principle and like the soul of tragedy.” (Poetics 1450a) For Aristotle, by contrast, a story is that which organizes the incidents of tragedy specifically—the dramatic form developed in ancient Athens, which peaked about 50 years before Aristotle started writing about it. Since the paradigmatic story only emerges as a late historical development, Campbell's strategy of establishing the form by cultural consensus won’t do. Aristotle’s claim hinges on what he calls the ἴδιον (defining quality) of tragedy: its capacity, “through pity and fear,” to induce catharsis (κάθαρσις) of these same feelings. “Pity and fear” had already been established at the time as states with which tragedy and display speeches were specially concerned. Spectator reactions to tragedy—and comedy, for that matter—were conspicuous. *** Aristotle attempts to explain how this effect actually works. The problem becomes salient once one appreciates that pity and fear are almost opposites, and from many angles drive the other out. Fear concerns our own anticipation of pain; pity concerns our anticipation of the suffering of others who are like us—so long as they don’t deserve it. (Rhet 2.5; 2.7; cf. Poetics 13). It’s this intersubjective asymmetry—the lack of a “doesn’t deserve it” clause on fear, in contrast to pity—which tragedy exploits. In Chapter 9, Aristotle locates a case where fear and pity come to be “on account of one another,” and so “contrary to expectation.” “…if this is the case, it will be more wondrous than if they come to be spontaneously or by chance, since even among chance things those seem most wondrous which appear to come to be as if for a purpose. For example, the statue of Mitys at Argo killed the one who was the cause of the death of Mitys; it fell on him while he contemplated it. For it is likely that as things of this sort do not come to be at random, so stories of this sort are necessarily more beautiful.” (Emphasis added). The mutual generation of pity-fear (exemplified in Mitys) produces a paradoxical effect with wondrous impact. This, Aristotle explains in Chapter 11, is most powerfully induced by way of an ideally-simultaneous moment of reversal and recognition. These, say Aristotle, besides the story, “are the greatest things by which tragedy guides the soul (psuchagogei),” and specifically characterize a “complex” story against a “simple” one. Aristotle gives as an example of reversal from Sophocles’ Oedipus, regarded as a tragic masterpiece: “For example in the Oedipus, on his coming [the messenger from Corinth], in supposing that he was going to cheer Oedipus and release him from the fear regarding his mother, once he had made it clear who he was, he did the contrary.” And an explanation of recognition, also from the Oedipus, at the same time (presumably by Oedipus) as he suddenly sees the world anew. “Recognition (anagnorisis), on the other hand, just as the name too signifies, is a change from ignorance to knowledge, whether toward friendship or enmity, of those whose relation to good or ill fortune has already been determined.” Since recognition and reversal concern mistakes, and mistakes are made by people acting in their world, the mistake that induces recognition/reversal depends on an appropriate agent to make it. Aristotle attempts to determine this agent in Chapter 13. He begins by making what would seem to be a few elementary exclusions. The tragic hero can’t be an evil man, whether he would prosper by the end, or receive his comeuppance. In the first case, the spectator would find it entirely unsatisfying. In the second, some feeling of “philanthropia” would at least be generated. Aristotle adds another caveat: “…it is clear that sound men ought not to be shown changing from good to bad fortune (for this is neither fearful nor pitiable but disgusting (miaron))…” “Sound,” here is a technical term, “epiekes,” defined in the Nicomachean Ethics, meaning he who does not necessarily demand the full enactment of justice due to him by the law. Aristotle indicates that, beyond ineffective and unhappy, this would be “disgusting.” So, what remains is he: …who is neither distinguished by virtue and justice nor changing to bad fortune on account of vice and wickedness is of this sort, but one who changes on account of some mistake and is one of those in great repute and of good fortune such as Oedipus, Thyestes, and notable men of families of this sort. (Ch. 13) Ergo, Oedipus is a paradigmatic tragic hero, and Oedipus (presumably) the paradigmatic story, at least in its general design. The spectator begins by identifying with a hero, in a similar way to how they’d identify with an athlete at a sporting event. (EN 1166b34-1167a6ff.; 1170b6-15. Friendship (philia) models the self's relation to others: “all the things that have to do with friendship arise out of oneself and extend to others” (1168b3-7). To judge that someone is a philos is to be aware that their being is similar to one's own (1170b7-13). A philos, then, provides a second self, but one whose ways of being-at-work can be observed from a detached perspective not easily attained with regard to one's own being (1169b34-36).) But as the events of the play unfold, And yet, we pity them. Why? The two axes of identification (as self and as other) are driven to an intersection so that we experience them both at once. Normally, Aristotle tells us, pity is limited by the sufferer not deserving their suffering (Rhet 2.8). But something in tragedy causes us to reckon that perspective against our own identification with the protagonist, would that our circumstances be the same. For the protagonist to trigger the tragic effect, they would have to be in some way culpable; meaning, they would be much like the defendant in a case of involuntary manslaughter; who never meant to do what he did, but it happened anyway, because he was in some way negligent, relative to an expected standard of conduct. This is a mistake in the specific sense as defined by Aristotle in Nicomachean Ethics 5.8. And this realization is the occasion for the cathartic expression we associate with tragedy. Couldn’t the same happen to me if I was justly punished for my worst lapses, had the worst possible or probable consequence of those lapses come to pass? Might there be outcomes still? Would I then be worthy of any pity at all? In the moment of reversal/recognition, our daily, oscillating, tendencies of blame and forgiveness are momentarily suspended and we see things, for a moment, in a personally significant reflection upon the whole. “At what point could I be forgiven? At what point is it just a matter of time?” “Was I too loose? Was I too tight? What could have been, what if not for… What might be yet to come, unless I’m diligent and prepared…” This node of human anxieties, those of the “akratic” (unrestrained person) (more or less), are those projected upon stories and salved by tragedy, in due proportion to the matter concerned (ranging from day-to-day worries to full-blown mania).]] But critically, in Chapter 14, in an apparent act of meta-narrative, Aristotle conspicuously reverses himself (see Benardete/Davis). He produces a new analysis of the best tragedies, with Sophocles’ Oedipus now ranking second place to Euripides’ Iphigenia at Taurus. But the strongest (c) is the last; I mean for example, in the Cresphontes, Merope intends to kill her son and does not kill him but recognizes him, and in the Iphigenia, the sister goes through this with her brother, and in the Helle, the son, intending to betray his mother, recognizes her. (1454a) These were tragedies, but among a small handful with happy endings, pioneered by Euripides, known formally as melodramas, in which the heroes narrowly avoid their proscribed fates. Aristotle found this variant no less “strong.” This means that the tragedy effect doesn’t necessarily depend on the tragic mistake actually coming to pass, per se. As well, this means that the paradigmatic tragedy does not necessarily have a conventionally “tragic” ending. But at what point does a melodrama become a comedy properly? Comedy was just a modification of the same imaginary power; seeing mistakes, made frequently, but of no consequence, whether scaled to the size of a joke, or tragedy, or epic (like Homer’s lost Margites). Aristotle makes the geometrical symmetry between the forms clear: For Margites stands in analogy: just as the Iliad and the Odyssey are related to tragedies so is this related to comedies. (Ch 4—on media histories to that point). But Aristotle never says this: that tragedy, let alone tragedy ending in disaster, was best, per se. Aristotle writes at the end of Chapter 13: “For the poets follow the spectators, making their poetry as they would pray for things to be. But this is not the pleasure of tragedy but is rather proper to comedy, where those who are the greatest of enemies in the story, for example, Orestes and Aegisthus, having become friends, go off at the end, and no one is killed by anyone.” The poets are motivated by the spectators to pander to their desire for things to end the way they would wish for them to be, rather than as they are. It is just this narrow technical space which pertains to our question above regarding Spielberg. To what extent is narrative optimism a narcotic? Given that Hollywood film typically has obvious political significances and relevancies, fathoming the relevance of this question requires the rhetorical lens. The two forms are fairly easily converted, as Aristotle points out in Chapter 19: “And it is clear that one should make use of the same forms in the events as well whenever one ought to produce pitiful things, terrible things, great things, or likely things, except that it differs in this way, that the one should be made apparent without being didactic, while other should be produced in speech by the speaker and should come to be as a consequence of speech.” Aristotle counts among the means of persuasion not only the argument itself, and the state of mind the listeners are put into, but also the character of the speaker. Considering the character of the speaker, Aristotle says three things matter: “These are judgement, virtue, and goodwill. For people go wrong in the things they speak of or give advice about by reason of all these or of any one of them, since they either have incorrect opinions on account of a lack of judgement, or while having correct opinions do not say what seems true to them on account of vice, or, if they are of good judgment and decent but not good-willed, it is possible for that very reason for them to fail to give the best advice they know how to give; besides these there are none. It is a necessity, therefore, that anyone who seems to have all these attributes will be trusted by his hearers.” The obvious problem is that when a speaker’s lived behaviour contradicts the ethical implications in his speech (should they exist), the former is at least a factor in the spectatorship of the work. The real difference is this, that one tells what happened and the other what might happen. For this reason poetry is something more scientific and serious than history, because poetry tends to give general truths while history gives particular facts.
    Aristotle, On Poetics — 1451b, trans. Sachs

    If Aristotle is right about the priority of poetry to history when it comes to truth, then a civilization's poetry is in fact a primary means of its access to truth. But what happens to our stories when we void the moral claims implicit inside of them?
